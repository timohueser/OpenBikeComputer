A · Map first

The planner riders already know from Komoot and Ride with GPS, with our rules applied well. The map is the work surface, a side panel holds the route, and the profile opens on request. The query box is the search field; its answers replace the panel until the rider goes back to the route.

Strong

  • At home on the website: it is the planner riders already know. Points panel, whole-trip profile with the days on one line, layers, versions and export in the bar.
  • On the road: the map is the screen, and the options and the amber Send to device are in thumb reach.
  • The panel footer keeps the preset and the trip figures under any answer, so the rider never loses the route.

Weak

  • The box is at the top of the phone screen, far from the thumb. A second query needs two hands.
  • Answers replace the 360 px panel, so comparing a result with the day list means going back and forth.
  • In the tent the answer is a sheet over most of the map. The profile is secondary, so there is no natural place for the day window or for dragging a day end.

B · Profile first

The route is one line from start to end, so its natural shape is a strip. The profile strip is the main editing surface: it holds the days, surface, gaps, snow and the answers of the box, and the rider drags day ends on it. The map follows the strip and is used for new points and fine edits.

Strong

  • At home: ten days on one line. Lengths, climbs, snow, closures and the answers are in one view, and dragging a day end is the cheapest edit there is.
  • In the tent: tomorrow as a strip with the 5–6 h window answers “where do I sleep” before the rider reads the list. The mock selects the campsite inside the window, not the literal answer.
  • The gaps row stays on the strip, empty until asked, so the feature has a visible home without pushing warnings.

Weak

  • On the road: a 190 pt strip at the top is out of thumb reach and adds little to a 34 km A→B choice. The map gets only about 190 pt.
  • On the website the map is 740 × 330 px, so it is not the main surface. New points and the hotel detour are map edits, and the strip can show “1.1 km off the line” only as a tick.

C · Query first

One box drives the planning. Places, questions about the route and changes to it all go through the box, and each answer is one fixed card. The map is for looking and for fine edits by hand. The plan itself is a slim summary that opens into the days and the profile.

Strong

  • On the road: one word in a bottom box, one card, one amber button, and a clean map.
  • In the tent: the empty box offers requests that fit the plan (Places to sleep · end of Day 4), so one tap answers the job and teaches what the box can do.
  • The import job is one request: via Hotel Krone St. Peter, then Apply.

Weak

  • Everything that is not a sentence is one step further away: the days, the profile, the day ends, the point types, the segment modes and the snow history.
  • The answer card covers the centre of the map, where the rider wants to look.
  • Ten days of planning through 80-character requests is slow. A box that answers with a card is the shape people now link with assistants; the fixed cards and the missing history reduce that risk but do not remove it.

D · Map and strip Recommended

The map-first layout riders know, with the profile strip always on screen, as in the trip day editor. The box is more than a search field: a request is always what and where. The rider can type it, or point at a day end, a point or a stretch to fill the where part and then choose what. Answers show in the list, on the strip and on the map at once.

Strong

  • In the tent and at home: the days, the day window, the gaps and the answers all land on the strip, so “where do I sleep tomorrow” reads in one glance. List, strip tick and map marker are linked.
  • Import: the line stays untouched, the detour is a ghost until Apply, and the data the rider asked for appears as strip rows, never as a pop-up.
  • “Gaps on request” and “Keep on strip” turned out to be one mechanism: a row the rider asked for, which stays until its × is tapped.

Weak

  • On the road: box, strip, options and the amber button need a 530 pt sheet, which leaves about 214 pt of map. A fix to try: fold the strip into the selected option’s row on this screen.
  • The strip costs 170 px of map on the website in every state, also for a rider who never edits days.
  • Pointing needs a visible row of choices after the tap (“Also here”), or the mechanism is invisible. On the phone it did not fit next to the list, so the tent mock shows the typed path.

The first mock is an extra: the website at rest, with no request, because a permanent strip must also be judged when nothing is asked.

The rider jobs as visible states

Each job is written as what the rider sees and does, step by step, in direction D. The last line of each job says where the other directions differ.

On the road: “Get me to Titisee”

iPhone, one hand, weak signal. Here is Glottertal. Touring bike.

  1. The planner opens on the map at the rider’s position. The box is at the top of the sheet, in thumb reach.
  2. The rider types Titisee. Matching places appear while they type: Titisee, village, 23 km; Titisee-Neustadt, town.
  3. They tap Titisee. One place card shows its kind and distance, with Route from here and Add to route.
  4. They tap Route from here. The map shows three lines. The sheet names each option by what it wins: Shortest, 34 km, 1,020 m, 2 h 50. Least climbing, +13 km, −300 m. Least unpaved, +2 km, +40 m.
  5. The strip of the selected option shows its 3.1 km unpaved, of which 1.4 km is unknown surface.
  6. They tap Send to device. The route goes to the OBC over Bluetooth. Without a connection, search and routing still work in downloaded areas.

A puts the box at the top of the screen, far from the thumb. B gives the map less room. C shows the same card, from the same box at the bottom.

In the tent: “Where do I sleep tomorrow?”

iPhone, the evening of Day 3 in Val d’Isère. Tomorrow is Day 4, Sunday 20 June.

  1. The rider opens the trip. The sheet shows the strip and the days. Day 4: 104 km, 2,310 m, 7 h 10 riding. Too long after a hard Day 3.
  2. They tap the Day 4 end on the strip. The box shows End of Day 4 · Valloire and offers Places to sleep, Water, Shops.
  3. They drag the Day 4 end to the left. The day figures change while they drag. The line does not change. They release near Saint-Michel-de-Maurienne: 88 km, 5 h 20.
  4. They tap Places to sleep. The list shows the campsites near the new end, each with its km, distance off the line and extra climb. They tap Camping Le Petit Nice and then End Day 4 here.
  5. Day 5 now shows 69 km and 6 h 20, and starts with the Col du Télégraphe. One quiet line says so. Undo is one tap.
  6. They type shops open sunday day 4. The list shows each shop’s Sunday hours: Carrefour Market, Modane, Sun 8:30–12:30. Intermarché, Saint-Michel, Sun closed.

B does the same on a larger strip, with a smaller map. A and C hide the days in a list, so the rider types or scrolls instead of dragging.

At home: “Ten days through the Alps”

Website, several evenings. Touring bike.

  1. The rider types Genève and taps Start here, then Nice and Route to here. The planner shows the A→B route with its alternatives.
  2. They add passes: via Col du Galibier gives a proposal card with its cost; Apply adds a Pass here point. A new point never changes the order of the others.
  3. They type 10 days or use Split into days. The proposal places the day ends at about 5 h of riding each. The strip shows the days; the panel lists them.
  4. They refine: drag day ends on the strip; ask campsites end of day 4; ask water gaps and add the marker “Last water for 29 km”. The strip shows a seasonal closure mark and the snow history on the Col de l’Iseran.
  5. They drag across the unknown surface on Day 2 on the strip and choose Other ways for this stretch. Only that stretch changes.
  6. They set the trip dates in the header (start Thu 17 Jun, rest day 6). Each save makes a version under Versions. Continue on phone plays the animated QR code for the phone.

A shows the same steps with the profile in a panel the rider opens. B makes the days easier and the new points harder. C does most steps by sentence, and the rider sees less of the plan at once.

Import: “The published route, but via my hotel”

Website or iPhone. A GPX file, “Schwarzwald Gravel, 3 days”.

  1. The rider taps Import and chooses the file. The planner shows the line exactly as it is in the file, with the label “Imported line · not re-routed”, and its figures: 312 km, 6,480 m.
  2. The strip shows our data on their line: 41 km unknown surface. The line itself does not change.
  3. They type via Hotel Krone St. Peter. A proposal card says: Add a visit, +2.3 km, +60 m, only the detour is routed, 312 km of the line unchanged. The detour shows as a ghost line on the map.
  4. They tap Apply. The line leaves the imported line at its nearest point, visits the hotel and returns to the same point.
  5. They ask water gaps: “No mapped water for 38 km” on Day 2. Add marker puts a normal waypoint on the line, which the device shows in What’s next.
  6. They tap Send to device.

All four directions handle this well. The proposal card is the same in each; D and B also mark the detour on the strip.

The query box

A request has three parts: what, where and filters. After “Understood as”, the box shows each part as a chip, so the rider sees what it will do before it does anything. The answer is always one of four cards, each with fixed actions. The states below use the grammar of direction D.

  • The box never acts on a guess. A change to the route waits for Apply, and Undo reverses it like any other edit.
  • Words it does not understand stay visible, struck through, and are ignored. If it understands nothing, it says so and shows the place search results for the text.
  • Pointing builds the same request as typing. A tap on a day end, a point or a stretch fills the where chip. Pointing and place search need no model, so they work even before the model has downloaded.
  • “Open on Sunday” needs no trip dates: it filters on the Sunday hours. “Open on Day 4” needs the date of Day 4, so without dates the box asks for them in one quiet line. Issue #2236 currently says the Sunday case needs dates.
  • Offline, everything works in downloaded areas. The box says when a part of the request lies outside them.

The model behind the box

The box needs a model for one job only: to turn a typed sentence into a request. What the box accepts and what it must return decide which model fits. The speed and size of the models we can run decide in turn how free the sentence can be. This section sets out both sides and proposes an experiment.

How a sentence becomes an answer

Sentencecampsites end of day 4At most 80 characters.
Parser (the model)Turns the words into a request. It knows nothing about the trip.
Requestplaces · campsite · end of day 4 · 5 kmShown to the rider as chips.
Resolver (our code)“End of Day 4” becomes km 317 of the trip, Valloire. “Kandel” becomes a place from the index.
Answer (our data)The place index in the map cells, the router and the plan.

The parser only returns references: “day 4”, “end”, “Kandel”, “here”. Our own code looks them up in the plan and the place index. So the parser needs no trip data in its input, the input stays at about 10 to 30 tokens, and the model can stay small. Everything the rider sees as a result comes from our data, so the model cannot invent a place.

Pointing at the strip or the map builds the request directly, without the parser. Place search also works without it. If the model is not downloaded yet, the box still works for names and for pointing.

  • Where: here, the map view, the route, Day N, start, middle or end of Day N, tomorrow, near a place, between two places, km A to B, this stretch.
  • What: the seven kinds in the map cells (water, campsite, lodging, resupply, pharmacy, bike shop, train) and their sub-kinds (supermarket, bakery, hut, hostel, fountain, spring). The words in each language come from the term lists of the iD tagging schema.

What goes in: how free can the sentence be?

Level 1Fixed forms
campsites end of day 4 · route to Titisee

Fixed word order, synonyms allowed. Hand-written rules can do it. You rejected fixed phrases in round 4.

Level 2Proposed for v1
where can I camp at the end of day 4 · wo kann ich am Ende von Tag 4 zelten · any supermarket open sunday tomorrow

Any wording of one request, in several languages, with typos. One what, one where and a few filters.

Level 3Combined
campsites near a supermarket at the end of day 4 · end day 4 in saint-michel and find a campsite there

Two things related to each other, or two requests in one sentence. The chips must then show a nested request.

Level 4Out of scope
is the galibier worth it · will it rain on thursday

Open questions. The box says it does not understand and shows place search results.

The options

An encoder tagger reads the whole sentence in one pass and puts a label on each word: “campsites” is the what, “end” is the part of the day, “day 4” is the day. One more label gives the request type. It is fast, and its time does not depend on the length of the answer. It can only label words that are in the sentence, so it cannot write a value that is not there. Simple code joins the labels into the request.

A small generative model writes the request one token at a time, like autocomplete. It can express nested requests. Each token costs one pass through the model, so a 25-token request costs 25 passes, which makes it about ten times slower. A grammar lets it write only valid requests, but a valid request can still be wrong (“day 3” for “day 4”). A check that every value occurs in the sentence catches most of these.

What decides what

If the box must…then…
update the chips while the rider typesthe parse must take under about 50 ms: rules or an encoder. A generative model is too slow for every key.
understand combined requests (Level 3)a generative model, or a much richer tagger: about four times the download, and 1–2.5 s in browsers without WebGPU.
work on the website without a large downloadan encoder of about 60 MB, loaded on the first use of the box and then kept. A 240 MB model is too much for a visitor.
speak many languagesa multilingual model and training data in each language. Rules need hand work in each language.
work offline and without our serversthe parser runs on the phone and in the browser. A parser on a server is out.
use one code path on the phone and the websiteONNX Runtime runs the same encoder file on both. A Rust runtime (candle or tract) could keep the parser in the shared Rust core; its speed must be measured.

On public tests of this kind of task, a fine-tuned encoder and a fine-tuned small generative model score almost the same: on MASSIVE (51 languages), XLM-R base reaches 85.1 % intent accuracy and mT5-base 85.3 % (paper). On a narrow request language, both reach the high 90s after fine-tuning. Without fine-tuning, models under one billion parameters are weak. The largest effect is training data in each language: without it, whole-request accuracy drops by about 25 points.

Recommendation

  • Level 2 for v1, with a request language that can grow to Level 3 later without a new answer card.
  • mmBERT-small as a word tagger, with its vocabulary cut to English, German, French, Italian, Spanish and Dutch: about 60 MB, one file for the app and the website. Granite 4.0 350M with a grammar is the challenger, in case combined requests matter more than download size and speed.
  • English and German first.

Proposed experiment

  1. Write the request language above as a typed schema.
  2. Write a test set by hand: about 300 sentences in English and German and 50 each in French and Italian, each with its correct request. Include typos, free word order, Level 3 sentences and out-of-scope questions. You write part of it, so that it holds sentences that riders really type.
  3. Make training data: templates and word lists, then paraphrases written by a large model directly in each language. 5,000 to 20,000 examples per language. Remove examples with wrong labels.
  4. Train and compare the mmBERT-small tagger, Granite 4.0 350M with a grammar, and a rules-only baseline. LFM2.5-230M runs as a reference only, because of its licence.
  5. Measure whole-request accuracy; the share of wrong requests shown as understood, which is the worst error; the detection of out-of-scope sentences; parse time on a Mac, on an iPhone 12 or 13, and in Safari, Chrome and Firefox on a 2020 laptop without WebGPU; the download size.
  6. Pass bar (proposal): 95 % whole-request accuracy on English and German Level 2 sentences and 85 % in French and Italian; at most 2 % wrong requests shown as understood; at most 100 ms on an iPhone 12 and 300 ms in a browser without WebGPU; at most 80 MB download.

Effort: about 3 to 5 days of agent work on the Mac (estimate), about 2 GB of model downloads, and one session with your iPhone for the timing.
